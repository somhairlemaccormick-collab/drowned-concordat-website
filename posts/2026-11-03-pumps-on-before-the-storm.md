Storm season has properly started on the North Sea, so this week's Dispatch begins where the book does: in the Netherlands, with the water rising and the pumps already running.

## The Netherlands gets ready for Boelo

Two storms crossed the Netherlands in late October. Before they arrived, Rijkswaterstaat, the Dutch agency that runs the country's water defences, explained what it was doing. Its experts were watching from its water control room, the Waterkamer, in Lelystad. The big pumping station at IJmuiden was switched on early to lower water levels across the northern half of the Randstad, the country's crowded western heartland, so there would be room to store the coming rain. That was coordinated with the regional water boards. The agency also flagged a less obvious risk: with the Rhine still running extremely low, strong westerly winds could push salt water inland near Rotterdam.

Storm Boelo, the first officially named storm of the season, arrived that weekend with gusts of up to 115 km/h in places, including IJmuiden. DutchNews reported that a man was killed by a falling tree in Amsterdam and someone taking part in water sports was missing off Scheveningen. On Texel, seawater broke over the quays and flooded Oudeschild harbour. Separately, the agency noted that its yearly checks found the Maeslant and Oosterschelde barriers need extra attention and maintenance, the Oosterschelde mostly because of its age.

### Why it caught my eye

Everyone in the Delta calls the Bulwark "the wall", and the engineers keep correcting them: it is gates, locks and pumps. Here is the real version of that argument. Nobody waited for the storm to hit the defences. They pumped first, to make space, and worried about salt coming in the back door while the wind came in the front. A wall just stands there. A system has to think ahead.

## A glacier with less grip than we thought

Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica is often called the "Doomsday Glacier". A new study from the University of California, Irvine and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, published in Nature Communications, suggests it could add up to 30 mm to global sea levels by 2100. That is double earlier projections, and it assumes no additional warming.

The difference comes from the zone where the glacier leaves the seabed and starts to float. Older models treated that as a thin line. The new one treats it as a band that can be kilometres wide, where tides lift the ice and let warm seawater in underneath. With that added, the model matched the changes seen since the 1990s far better. The researchers put the chance of the glacier losing its grip on an undersea ridge that currently steadies it at about 60% by the end of the century.

### Why it caught my eye

Thirty millimetres sounds like nothing. But this is one glacier, and the point of the paper is that the ice is more sensitive than our tools assumed. The world of the book starts with a simple premise: the sea came, and stayed. It rarely arrives as one dramatic wave. It arrives as a revised estimate.

## Food is going to cost more

The World Bank's latest outlook, reported by Reuters, expects food commodity prices to rise by just over 10% this year and by more than 7% in 2027, nearly 18% across the two years. It points to disrupted grain shipments from the Black Sea, and expects fertiliser to rise 14% this year as urea prices jump. In a bad-case scenario, including a record El Niño, food prices could end up 12% higher than its central forecast. The Bank warns that could echo the early 2010s, when more than 40 million extra people fell into extreme poverty.

### Why it caught my eye

Behind the Bulwark, nobody worries about the price of grain because nobody eats it. The Council rations gel by the gram, and real food is so rare it is licensed by the Verdant Exchange. That sounds alien until you read a forecast like this one, where the price of a weekly shop depends on the weather in the Pacific and a shipping lane thousands of miles away.
