This week the water comes in on a calendar, and the chicken comes out of a steel tank. One story is about a coast racing to get ready, the other about a strange food that has quietly arrived on the menu.

## California braces for the king tides

King tides, the highest tides of the year, arrive along California's coast around Thanksgiving, and this year they have help. A strong El Niño has warmed the Pacific and pushed the sea itself a little higher, so tides have been running well above their predicted levels. KQED reports that in Corte Madera, on the Marin side of San Francisco Bay, bay water has been flowing around a row of homes for more than a month. Neighbours there are lining a dozen houses with stiff flood barriers, plastic sheeting and sandbags. One resident raised his whole house by four feet a few years ago. San Francisco's port has put temporary barriers along vulnerable stretches of the waterfront and is using wave buoys to see, in real time, where the water is pushing hardest.

Further south, Newport Beach is raising its sea walls in a hurry. ABC7 reports that last month a record high tide sent water over the walls and into homes and businesses on the Balboa Peninsula. The city checked 70 locations and found 40 stretches of wall that needed to go up by 12 to 24 inches. California's Coastal Commission relaxed permitting so the emergency work could go ahead, and the city aimed to finish before Thanksgiving. Its utilities director summed it up in two words: "It's unprecedented."

### Why it caught my eye

What struck me was how ordinary the tools are. Sandbags, plastic sheeting, a few extra inches of concrete, a buoy bobbing off a pier. And yet together they are a system in miniature: forecast, measure, raise, block, watch. That is how the engineers in the book think about the Bulwark, as gates, locks and pumps rather than one great wall. The other thing that stayed with me is the timetable. Nobody knows exactly where the next storm will land, but everyone knows the date of the next king tide. More are forecast for Christmas week and late January.

## Chicken from a tank

Upside Foods, one of the best-known cultivated meat companies, has launched its chicken across California foodservice, FoodNavigator reports. Restaurants, corporate campuses and university dining halls can now buy it. "Upside Chicken" is a blend: chicken grown from animal cells, mixed with soy protein, pea fibre and olive oil. The company says its 20,000-litre tanks can turn out more than a million pounds a year, and that it is selling to restaurants at prices comparable to premium organic chicken. Its chief executive puts the improvement down to cheaper cell nutrients and dropping serum from the growth medium. Supermarkets are the next goal.

The legal ground is uneven. Upside won US federal approval in 2023, but FoodNavigator lists eight states that have banned or restricted cultivated meat. A federal appeals court has upheld Florida's ban, and a challenge to Texas's two-year ban, brought by Upside and another company, has yet to be decided.

### Why it caught my eye

In the Delta, people live on gel, as foam or lump, and on ration bars from Axiom Chemical Synthesis, while real food is rare and licensed by the Verdant Exchange. Here the question runs the other way: can food grown in a tank become ordinary enough, and cheap enough, to sit next to the real thing? Upside's answer so far is a dish in a California restaurant. Whether it travels further may depend as much on lawmakers as on bioreactors.
