The wall is a system, and the menu is a gamble

London learns its sea is rising faster than it used to, and the companies betting on lab-grown meat are running short of money. Two real stories that could have come from behind the wall.

Welcome to Dispatches. Each week I’ll pick out a few real stories that read as if they had leaked through the Bulwark: the seas, the walls we build against them, and the strange business of deciding what people eat. No spoilers here, ever. Just the news, and a note on why it caught my eye.

London checks its wall

The Environment Agency has published its fifteen-year check-up on the plan that protects London and the Thames Estuary from the sea. The headline number is small but it isn’t comforting. Between 1993 and 2024, the estuary’s sea level rose by an average of just over 4 mm a year. The previous review, covering roughly 1990 to 2018, found a little over 3.6 mm. The rise is speeding up.

The more interesting detail is in the machinery. The defences themselves are in good shape: around 96% of the estuary’s walls and embankments meet their required condition. The weak point is prediction. The Thames Barrier closes on forecasts, and today those forecasts can be out by 30 to 40 cm. The goal is to get that down to 20 cm by 2030. If that doesn’t happen, the Barrier will have to close more often just to be safe, including on days when it turns out it didn’t need to.

Why it caught my eye

In the book, the engineers insist the Bulwark is not a wall. It’s a system of gates, locks, pumps and people, watched every hour of every day. The Thames review says the same thing in a civil servant’s voice. Concrete is the easy part. What keeps a city dry is the forecast, the maintenance schedule, and someone deciding when to close the gates.

The lab-grown meat bubble deflates

Food Safety News ran a long look at cultivated meat this month, and the picture is much less glossy than it was five years ago. Venture money has collapsed, from roughly $989 million in 2021 to about $36 million in 2025. Several companies have closed, including two Dutch ones: Meatable, which shut down late last year, and Upstream Foods, which was working on cultivated salmon fat. Believer Meats, once valued at $600 million, closed without ever producing anything and left behind an empty $125 million plant in North Carolina.

Regulators haven’t made it easier. Products have been approved in the US, Singapore, Australia, New Zealand, Israel and Hong Kong, but the European Food Safety Authority has yet to approve any for sale in the EU. Meanwhile at least eight US states have banned or heavily restricted cultivated meat, and South Dakota’s five-year moratorium began in July.

Why it caught my eye

The Delta eats gel, synthesised at industrial scale, because there is nothing else. Our world is running the experiment in the other direction. We have plenty of real food, and the synthetic kind is fighting for money, permission and appetite. Whether lab-grown food ever feeds anyone may depend less on chemistry than on who is allowed to sell it, and who decides they would rather ban it.
