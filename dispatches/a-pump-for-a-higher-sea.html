A pump for a higher sea, and protein from thin air

The Netherlands picks a design for a giant new pumping station at IJmuiden because the sea will soon be too high to drain by gravity, and Europe's food regulator backs a protein grown from carbon dioxide and electricity. Two real stories from the week.

This week the water story is about a door that is slowly closing, and the food story is about a meal made from the air. Neither involves a storm. Both feel a little closer to the Delta than they did a year ago.

IJmuiden gets ready to pump

At IJmuiden, where the North Sea Canal meets the coast, the Netherlands runs a complex of sluices and pumps that drains surplus water from a large part of the west of the country and helps keep the sea out. Parts of it are reaching the end of their working lives, so Rijkswaterstaat, the national water agency, has been studying what should replace them. On 10 November it announced the end of the third phase of that study, and a preferred design.

The plan is a new pumping station able to move 410 cubic metres of water a second, built mostly on the northern bank of the inner discharge canal and designed to last a hundred years, with room to grow. Three options were compared on reliability, cost, fish safety, disruption to shipping and more. The winner scored best on how easily it could be built in stages, because the old complex has to keep working throughout: the water still needs somewhere to go while the new station goes up. The minister has set aside money as part of a wider reshuffle of national infrastructure projects, and the next phase will turn the design into a formal project decision, including an environmental impact study.

Why it caught my eye

The reason for all this is in one plain sentence. As the sea rises, the sluice will no longer be able to let water out on its own, and everything will have to be pumped. For generations, low tide did some of the work for free. Here is an official plan for the day that stops. The Bulwark in the book is gates, locks and pumps for exactly this reason: once the sea outside sits higher than the land inside, a wall alone keeps nothing dry. Something has to lift the water over it, every hour, for good.

Protein from thin air

The European Food Safety Authority has given a positive scientific opinion to Solein, a protein powder made by the Finnish company Solar Foods. It is grown by fermenting a microbe first found in Finnish nature, in a bioreactor, fed on carbon dioxide and hydrogen and powered by electricity, then dried into a yellow powder. The company markets it as “protein from thin air”. EFSA found it safe under the proposed conditions of use, which Solar Foods says makes it the first ingredient made by gas fermentation to get such an opinion.

It is not on European shelves yet. The European Commission now has to draft the legal act and put it to a vote of member states, which the company expects to take seven to nine months. The application was filed just over five years ago. Solein is already sold in a few products in Singapore and the US, and its biggest shareholder, the Finnish food company Fazer, says it is ready to launch in Europe once permission comes. A much larger factory in Finland is planned, but depends on firm orders and full financing.

Why it caught my eye

The Delta eats gel, synthesised at industrial scale by Axiom Chemical Synthesis and handed out as foam or lump. Solein is nothing like gel, but the idea underneath is the same: food that doesn’t need fields, weather or animals, just a tank, a gas and a power supply. The company’s own pitch is predictable supply whatever the climate does. What struck me most was the timetable. The microbe was ready years ago. The long wait has been for a committee to say yes.
