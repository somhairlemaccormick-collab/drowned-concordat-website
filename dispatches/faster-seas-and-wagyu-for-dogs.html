Ireland's quickening tide, and lab-grown Wagyu for dogs

A century of Irish tide records shows the sea rising five times faster than it used to, and the UK clears cultivated beef for the first time, though only for pets. Two real stories from the week.

This week the sea came from an old ledger and the food came from a single cow that never had to die. Two stories, both quieter than a storm, and both stranger the longer you look at them.

A hundred years of Irish tides

Researchers at Maynooth University have pieced together a century of sea-level records from six tide gauges around Ireland: Belfast, Malin Head, Dublin Port, Cork, Galway and Tarbert on the Shannon Estuary. Some of the older Cork, Galway and Tarbert records had never been used in a national assessment before, so part of the work was simply recovering them. The study, published in the journal Ocean Science and reported by RTÉ, took around a decade.

The result is a clear acceleration. Between 1925 and 2000 the sea around Ireland rose by an average of 0.59 mm a year. Between 2000 and 2024 that average was 3.13 mm, more than five times faster, and by 2024 the yearly rate had reached about 4.26 mm, the quickest in the whole record. Put another way, the sea rose roughly 1.3 cm in the fifty years from 1925, and about 9 cm in the thirty years from 1993. The coast doesn’t feel it evenly, either. Land is still slowly rebounding from the last Ice Age in the north-east, so Belfast shows the smallest long-term rise and Tarbert in County Kerry the largest. The lead author’s summary was blunt: “Sea level is rising faster.”

Why it caught my eye

I love that this story starts in an archive. Someone had to go and find old tide readings, check them, and stitch them to modern instruments before anyone could say what the sea had been doing. The Delta lives behind the Bulwark because, somewhere back along the line, those numbers stopped being a slow curve and became a fact of daily life. The other detail I liked is the unevenness. A city a few hundred miles from another can face a very different sea, which is exactly why a defence ends up being a system of gates, locks and pumps tuned to its own coast, not one wall built to a single height.

Real beef, grown for the dog bowl

Oxford-based Ivy Farm Technologies has been cleared to sell its cultivated Wagyu beef as an ingredient for UK pet food. The company worked with Defra and the Animal and Plant Health Agency on the approval. Its meat is grown from cells taken from one registered Wagyu cow, with no slaughter involved, and is made at its Oxford site. In a small blind test run by a pet food maker, dogs took to a treat made with it as readily as one made with ordinary beef.

The first commercial-scale production run is due to finish in early 2027, and Ivy Farm has applied for a similar pet food approval in Europe. Approval to sell the same meat for people is still working its way through regulators in the UK, Europe and the US. For now, the first British animals to eat lab-grown Wagyu will have four legs.

Why it caught my eye

There’s a lovely irony here. In the Delta, real food is so rare that the Verdant Exchange has to license it, and most people live on gel and ration bars from Axiom Chemical Synthesis. Here, the engineered meat is the luxury, a premium ingredient for well-loved pets, while the paperwork for feeding it to humans is still in a queue. It is a reminder that who gets to eat something new is rarely just a question of whether it can be made. It is a question of who signs off, and in what order.
